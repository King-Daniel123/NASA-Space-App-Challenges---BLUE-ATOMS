The problem

Students learn the parts of a mission. They rarely get to trade them off.

Add one instrument and you add mass, power draw, data and cost. Each of those changes something else. That chain is what mission design is, and it stays abstract until you feel it.

  • Take a mission with a science goal, a cost cap and a deadline
  • Design the orbit, payload, spacecraft, power, radio and rocket
  • Face a review board that says what will fail, and why
  • Fly it, decide under pressure, and read the report
2 / 7
The core idea

Every choice is a trade, and the game shows the ripple.

Seven live gauges: cost, launch mass, power, data, science, schedule and odds. Change one thing and a line spells out what it did to all the others.

  • 15 instruments modelled on real ones, each with mass, power, cost, data and science value
  • 12 real launch vehicles, checked against the orbit and the spacecraft's mass
  • Real physics: the rocket equation, inverse-square sunlight and radio, energy balance in shadow
  • No single right answer: a 36 kg CubeSat and a nuclear-powered probe can both succeed

NASA open data at the centre of play

The real world sets the mission.

EONET Real wildfires, storms, floods and ice events are your targets. Where they are decides which orbit works. What they are sets the science priorities. Tomorrow the best design may be different.

DONKI Real solar flares and particle storms from the past year strike your spacecraft, each named in the log.

NeoWs / JPL The real Apophis flyby on 13 April 2029 is the deadline. The countdown shrinks every day.

GIBS NASA's Blue Marble is the map. ISROESAJAXACSA rockets and missions appear throughout.

4 / 7
Review, launch, operate

It tells you why, before and after.

A review board explains every problem before launch. Then the mission is built, launched and operated week by week, and the crew has to decide what to do when things go wrong.

5 / 7
Learning and playing together

Up to four players, four roles, four goals that pull apart.

  • Science Lead wants more instruments
  • Spacecraft Engineer wants mass and power margin
  • Comms and Ops Lead wants every bit sent home
  • Project Manager wants it on budget and on time

Each role owns part of the design, a go/no-go call before launch, and the in-flight decisions for its systems. The crew has to negotiate.

Also: three difficulty levels, a sandbox, example designs, a 27-term glossary, a ranked mission log, and share links that give another team the same design and the same luck.
